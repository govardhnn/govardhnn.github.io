Synthesis, Timing and Power

Logic Synthesis

Logic synthesis maps RTL to a target netlist: standard cells for ASICs; LUTs, flops, RAM, and DSP blocks for FPGAs.

  1. Elaborate parameters, generate blocks, and hierarchy.
  2. Optimize Boolean logic and arithmetic for area, timing, and power.
  3. Map to available cells or FPGA resources; NAND decomposition is not required.
  4. Optimize timing by resizing, buffering, and restructuring.
  5. Analyze timing under explicit constraints; repeat after placement and routing.

ASIC area may be expressed in gate equivalents (one GE is the area of a reference NAND2). FPGA area is usually reported by resource type.

// What you write: pure intent, no gate-level detail
module mux2 #(parameter W = 8) (
  input  logic [W-1:0] a, b,
  input  logic         sel,
  output logic [W-1:0] y
);
  assign y = sel ? b : a;
endmodule

The tool decides how that ternary becomes gates (AND-OR logic, a mux standard cell, or LUTs) from the target library and the timing constraints. Synthesis attempts to meet constraints; STA reports whether the constrained paths meet them under the analyzed models and corners.

Physical Implementation and Test

Placement assigns cells to sites; clock-tree synthesis distributes the clock; routing creates wires. Extracted wire resistance and capacitance feed back into timing and power analysis. Check electrical limits, timing across modes/corners, and physical design rules; a synthesized netlist alone is not a finished chip.

Design for test (DFT) makes manufactured faults observable. Scan connects state elements into shift chains: shift in a state, capture the circuit response, and shift it out. Automatic test-pattern generation targets fault models such as stuck-at and transition faults; memory BIST exercises arrays. Manufacturing test complements functional verification—it does not prove the design specification correct.

Combinational Timing

Every combinational circuit has two delay characteristics:

Term Definition
Propagation delay tpdt_{pd} maximum time from an input change until the outputs reach their final value
Contamination delay tcdt_{cd} minimum time from an input change until any output starts to change

tpdt_{pd} says how long to wait before trusting the output; tcdt_{cd} says how soon the old value stops being trustworthy. Between them the output can be anything.

Critical Path

Delays add along a path: the circuit’s tpdt_{pd} is the sum of tpdt_{pd} along the longest path (the critical path), and its tcdt_{cd} the sum along the shortest.

A circuit can be data critical (critical path through the data inputs, e.g. a wide mux chain) or control critical (through a select/control signal). Which one it is tells you where to optimize: register the control earlier, or restructure the data path.

Logical Effort: Reasoning About Gate Delay

STA reports that a path fails; logical effort explains why and predicts the best fix on the back of an envelope. Normalize delay to a process-dependent unit τ\tau; an inverter driving an identical inverter has d=2d=2 in this model:

d=gh+pd = g h + p

  • gg, logical effort: how much worse the gate’s topology is than an inverter at driving current, given equal input capacitance. Independent of size.
  • h=Cout/Cinh = C_{out}/C_{in}, electrical effort (fanout): the load it drives relative to its own input.
  • pp, parasitic delay: the gate driving its own diffusion capacitance; irreducible by sizing.
Gate gg pp
inverter 11 11
NAND2 4/34/3 22
NAND3 5/35/3 33
NOR2 5/35/3 22

These estimates assume a simple CMOS sizing model; actual delay depends on the library, input transition, and load.

For a path of NN stages, multiply the efforts: G=∏giG = \prod g_i, H=Cout/CinH = C_{out}/C_{in} for the whole path, BB for branching, giving path effort F=GBHF = GBH. Delay is minimized when every stage bears equal effort f̂=F1/N\hat{f} = F^{1/N}:

Dmin=NF1/N+∑piD_{min} = N F^{1/N} + \sum p_i

Worked example: inverter → NAND2 → inverter driving 8x the path’s input capacitance. G=1⋅43⋅1=43G = 1 \cdot \tfrac{4}{3} \cdot 1 = \tfrac{4}{3}, B=1B = 1, F=43⋅8=10.7F = \tfrac{4}{3} \cdot 8 = 10.7, so f̂=10.71/3=2.2\hat{f} = 10.7^{1/3} = 2.2 and D=3(2.2)+(1+2+1)=10.6τD = 3(2.2) + (1 + 2 + 1) = 10.6\,\tau. Sizing falls out backwards from the load: each stage’s input capacitance is Cin=g⋅Cout/f̂C_{in} = g \cdot C_{out} / \hat{f}.

Sequential Timing

A flip-flop has its own timing aperture around the clock edge:

Term Definition
tsetupt_{setup} data stable at least this long before the edge
tholdt_{hold} data stable at least this long after the edge
tpcqt_{pcq} clock-to-Q propagation delay (max)
tccqt_{ccq} clock-to-Q contamination delay (min)

tsetup+tholdt_{setup} + t_{hold} is the aperture time: data must hold steady through that whole window for a reliable capture.

The Setup Constraint (Max-Delay)

For two flip-flops joined by logic of delay tpdt_{pd}, sharing a clock of period TcT_c: Tc≥tpcq+tpd+tsetup⇔tpd≤Tc−(tpcq+tsetup) T_c \ge t_{pcq} + t_{pd} + t_{setup} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad t_{pd} \le T_c - (t_{pcq} + t_{setup}) tpcq+tsetupt_{pcq} + t_{setup} is the sequencing overhead, clock budget lost to the flops before any logic runs. Negative setup slack means the design cannot run at that frequency; fixes include faster logic, pipelining, clock-path adjustment, or a slower clock.

The Hold Constraint (Min-Delay)

The same pair also imposes a lower bound, independent of clock period: tccq+tcd≥thold t_{ccq} + t_{cd} \ge t_{hold} If the path is too fast, data launched by an edge races through and corrupts the capture of the previous cycle’s data at the same edge. A hold violation cannot be fixed by slowing the clock; the fix is adding delay (buffers) on the offending path. Even direct flop-to-flop connections require hold analysis using library delays and clock skew.

Pipelining

When a combinational block’s tpdt_{pd} exceeds the setup ceiling, split it with a register in the middle: two faster blocks, each meeting timing at a shorter period.

// Before: one combinational block, too slow for the target period
assign mid = heavy_function_1(a);
assign y   = heavy_function_2(mid);

// After: a pipeline register splits the work across two cycles
always_ff @(posedge clk) mid_reg <= heavy_function_1(a);
assign y = heavy_function_2(mid_reg);

An added register increases latency by one cycle. Shorter stages may increase clock frequency and throughput, but each stage adds register area, clock power, and sequencing overhead.

Worked Example: Pipelining a 32-bit Ripple-Carry Adder

Each full-adder stage has carry-in-to-carry-out delay tdcc=100pst_{dcc} = 100\,\text{ps}, so a 32-bit ripple adder needs tadd=32×tdcc=3.2ns⇒≈312M adds/s. t_{add} = 32 \times t_{dcc} = 3.2\,\text{ns} \;\Rightarrow\; \approx 312\,\text{M adds/s}. For a target of 1 G adds/s (one per ns), split into four 8-bit slices, each followed by a register: 8×100ps=800ps8 \times 100\,\text{ps} = 800\,\text{ps} per slice, leaving 200 ps for sequencing overhead.

Stage Computes Also carries forward
1 bits 7:0 and carry into bit 8 upper operands
2 bits 15:8 and carry into bit 16 upper operands, low 8 sum bits
3 bits 23:16 and carry into bit 24 upper operands, low 16 sum bits
4 bits 31:24 and final carry low 24 sum bits

Register every row’s outputs. Each transaction reaches the result after four capture edges, with one new transaction accepted per edge. Delay operands, completed sum bits, and valid together; resetting the valid pipeline is sufficient when invalid data remains unobservable.

The Loop-Carried Dependency Problem

Register insertion works on feedforward paths. It does not work on feedback:

always_ff @(posedge clk or posedge reset)
  if (reset) acc <= '0;
  else       acc <= acc + data_in;   // depends on its own previous value

Simply inserting a register in this feedback path changes the recurrence. Preserve one update per cycle by redesigning the arithmetic or applying a proven transformation such as lookahead; otherwise accept a lower update rate.

Clock Skew

Real clock networks do not deliver the edge everywhere at once. For a launch flop A and capture flop B, tskew=tclk→B−tclk→A. t_{skew} = t_{clk \to B} - t_{clk \to A}. Positive skew (capture clock late) helps setup and hurts hold: Tc≥tpcq+tpd+tsetup−tskewtccq+tcd≥thold+tskew T_c \ge t_{pcq} + t_{pd} + t_{setup} - t_{skew} \qquad t_{ccq} + t_{cd} \ge t_{hold} + t_{skew} Useful skew delays a capture edge to improve setup at the cost of hold margin and timing on adjacent paths. Clock-tree design controls these arrival times; STA checks both constraints.

Skew vs. Jitter

Clock skew Clock jitter
Nature spatial: different flops see the edge at different times temporal: the same clock’s edges wander cycle to cycle
Source unequal clock-tree paths and loads PLL noise, supply (di/dt) noise, thermal
Constant? roughly static for a given layout random, time-varying
Useful? can be intentional (useful skew) never, pure margin loss

Before clock-tree implementation, uncertainty may budget estimated skew and jitter. With propagated clocks, STA models clock-path skew explicitly; uncertainty covers remaining margins without double-counting it.

Metastability

If a flop’s input changes inside its aperture, the output can settle into a metastable state, between logic levels, for an unbounded (usually short) time before randomly resolving. This is unavoidable when a signal crosses clock domains or arrives asynchronously: nothing guarantees the receiving flop’s aperture is respected. The mitigation is a 2-flop synchronizer: the first flop may go metastable, but it gets roughly one cycle, less clock-to-Q, routing, and setup overhead, to resolve before the second samples it.

MTBF

The synchronizer reduces the probability of a propagated metastable event, quantified as mean time between failures: MTBF=etr/τT0⋅fclk⋅fdata \text{MTBF} = \frac{e^{\,t_r/\tau}}{T_0 \cdot f_{clk} \cdot f_{data}} where trt_r is the resolution time allowed (about one period minus setup), τ\tau and T0T_0 are flop metastability constants, and fclkf_{clk}, fdataf_{data} are the sampling and input-change rates. The exponential is why adding a flop helps so dramatically, and why fast clocks and busy inputs hurt; at high frequency a 3-flop chain is sometimes needed to push MTBF to years or centuries.

Clock Domain Crossing (CDC)

A 2-flop synchronizer reduces metastability risk for a single-bit level held long enough to be sampled. Short pulses need stretching, toggle encoding with a rate limit, or a handshake. Synchronizing each bit of a bus independently lets different bits resolve on different edges, so the receiver can see a value that was never sent. The technique depends on what crosses:

What’s crossing Technique Why it works
stable single-bit level 2-flop synchronizer allows metastability to resolve; may miss short pulses
multi-bit control mux-recirculation / multi-cycle path: sync a 1-bit enable, hold the bus stable only the enable is synchronized; the bus is stable when sampled
counter / pointer register Gray code, synchronize each bit, constrain bus skew adjacent source values change one bit; constraints prevent transitions from different source steps overlapping
streaming data asynchronous FIFO, Gray-coded pointers decouples the domains; pointers cross safely as Gray codes
handshake req/ack, each synchronized into the other domain any width, at round-trip latency cost

Choose a crossing protocol that preserves the meaning of the whole value. Independently synchronized ordinary binary bits do not provide that guarantee.

Reset Strategy

Reset release has the same metastability hazard. Two disciplines:

Synchronous reset Asynchronous reset
Applied sampled only at the clock edge immediate, always_ff @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n)
+ timed as data; a glitch overlapping the edge can still be captured works with no clock running (safe at power-up)
− needs a running clock; adds a term to the data path glitch-susceptible; release is a timing hazard

Recovery and removal are the reset-release analogues of setup and hold: reset must release a minimum time before the edge (recovery) or stay asserted a minimum time after (removal), else the flop can go metastable. The standard fix asserts asynchronously but releases synchronously:

// Async-assert, sync-deassert reset synchronizer
logic rff1, rff2;
always_ff @(posedge clk or negedge rst_n_async) begin
  if (!rst_n_async) {rff2, rff1} <= 2'b00;        // async assert
  else              {rff2, rff1} <= {rff1, 1'b1}; // sync release, 2 edges
end
assign rst_n_sync = rff2;   // one synchronizer per destination clock domain

Glitch-prone reset pads additionally get a glitch filter or Schmitt-trigger input, since an async reset fires on any pulse meeting the flop’s minimum reset width.

Static Timing Analysis in Practice

STA applies the setup/hold equations exhaustively to every path, across all conditions, with no simulation vectors. It is the signoff answer to “does the chip meet timing”.

Slack

The per-path number STA reports: setup slack=(Tc+tskew−tsetup−tuncertainty)−(tpcq+tpd) \text{setup slack} = (T_c + t_{skew} - t_{setup} - t_{uncertainty}) - (t_{pcq} + t_{pd}) Slack ≥0\ge 0 passes; negative slack is a violation. Worst negative slack (WNS) and total negative slack (TNS) summarize how badly and how widely a design misses. Closing timing means driving all slack non-negative with faster cells, fewer levels, pipelining, useful skew, or a lower frequency.

Timing Paths and Constraints (SDC)

Clocks and I/O timing are declared in an SDC (Synopsys Design Constraints) file; STA then checks four path groups:

Path group From → to
reg → reg launch flop → capture flop
in → reg input port → first flop (set_input_delay)
reg → out last flop → output port (set_output_delay)
in → out pure combinational feed-through

Timing Exceptions

Not every topological path is a real timing path; undeclared exceptions cause false violations and over-constrained synthesis:

Exception Meaning Example
false path exempt from ordinary setup/hold analysis logically impossible paths or validated asynchronous crossings (which may still need CDC delay/skew constraints)
multicycle path protocol guarantees extra settling cycles coordinated launch/capture enables; configure the corresponding hold check
set_case_analysis pin constant in this mode, prune through it a test-mode mux select

An exception must reflect a real functional guarantee. False paths may toggle (e.g. CDC paths); their correctness must follow from the protocol and remaining constraints.

Corners and On-Chip Variation

Timing must hold at every manufacturing and operating condition, so STA runs at multiple PVT corners (process, voltage, temperature):

Check Worst corner Why
setup (max delay) often slow process, low V, high T largest data delay
hold (min delay) often fast process, high V, low T smallest data delay

Temperature inversion and clock/data path interactions can change the worst corner; analyze all required library corners.

On-chip variation (OCV) derating adds pessimism for device-to-device differences on one die. Signoff is multi-corner multi-mode (MCMM): every corner crossed with every functional mode (mission, test, low-power), which is why timing closure is a large part of tapeout cost.

Power and Energy

Power constrains cooling and supply capacity; energy constrains battery life. The main components are:

Component Spent Scales with
dynamic only while signals switch α⋅C⋅VDD2⋅f\alpha \cdot C \cdot V_{DD}^2 \cdot f
static (leakage) continuously, even idle transistor leakage, worse as devices shrink

Dynamic Power

Switching a node charges its load capacitance from the supply and later dumps the charge to ground, unrecovered: Pdyn=αCVDD2f P_{dyn} = \alpha \, C \, V_{DD}^2 \, f

Term Meaning Lever
α\alpha activity factor: expected number of 0→1 transitions per cycle (1 for the clock, 0 when static) clock/power gating, fewer glitches
CC switched capacitance (gate + diffusion + wire) smaller cells, shorter wires, fewer levels
VDDV_{DD} supply voltage the quadratic term, the strongest lever
ff clock frequency run no faster than the workload needs

Halving VDDV_{DD} quarters dynamic power, which is why voltage scaling dominates low-power design.

For independent successive samples, α=P0P1\alpha=P_0P_1; equiprobable bits give 0.250.25. Glitches add transitions. Short-circuit power is additional current while pull-up and pull-down networks briefly conduct together; it depends on slew, load, and technology.

Static (Leakage) Power

Leakage consumes power even when clocks and data stop switching.

Source Cause
subthreshold current through an “off” transistor; exponentially worse as VtV_t drops
gate tunneling through the thin gate dielectric (mitigated by high-k)
junction reverse-biased junction current (BTBT, GIDL)

The stack effect: a series stack of off transistors can leak substantially less than one off transistor, so a gate’s leakage depends on its input values; idle gates can be parked in low-leakage input states.

Clock Gating

The clock has α=1\alpha=1 and often drives large capacitance. Clock gating stops clock transitions in idle blocks:

always_ff @(posedge clk) if (en) q <= d;   // register enable; mapping depends on flow

For an AND-gated clock, hold the enable stable while the clock is high. An integrated clock-gating (ICG) cell typically uses a low-transparent latch before the AND; avoid an unconstrained combinational gate on the clock.

Style How the ICG gets there
inferred enabled registers may map to ICG cells when ASIC clock-gating optimization is enabled
instantiated instantiate the library ICG cell by hand (explicit, library-specific)

On FPGAs, prefer dedicated register enables or clock-control resources; an enable does not automatically stop the clock tree.

Gating high in the distribution tree turns off whole branches of the high-activity clock network (most savings); fine-grained per-register-bank gating saves less each but catches more idle cases.

Data (Operand) Gating

The combinational counterpart: hold an expensive block’s inputs steady on cycles when its result is discarded, so its internal nodes never toggle.

assign a_g  = use_mult ? a : '0;
assign b_g  = use_mult ? b : '0;
assign prod = a_g * b_g;  // inputs stay constant while disabled; gating edges still switch

Both gating styles exploit the same rule: if a result is not used, do not pay to compute it.

Power Gating

A sleep transistor disconnects an idle block from a supply rail, greatly reducing leakage (a clock-gated block still leaks). Costs wake-up latency and area, and the block loses state, so it suits coarse-grained, long-idle blocks.

Multiple Threshold Voltages (Multi-Vt)

Low-Vt cells are faster but leak more; high-Vt cells save leakage where timing slack allows.

Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS)

Exploits the V2V^2 term at runtime: lower the frequency to the workload’s minimum, then lower the voltage to the minimum supporting that frequency. Under the limited approximation V∝fV\propto f, Pdyn∝V2f∝rate3, P_{dyn} \propto V^2 f \propto \text{rate}^3, This cubic scaling is only a rough model; voltage floors, leakage, and nonlinear delay limit it. A controller monitors load and temperature and picks the (V, f) point from the chip’s characterized table.

Power Intent: Domains and UPF (IEEE 1801)

Power gating and independent voltage control require power domains that RTL alone does not describe. Multi-Vt cell selection alone does not require separate domains. Power intent is captured separately in UPF (Unified Power Format, IEEE 1801), the way SDC carries timing intent. A design divides into power domains, each on, off, or at its own voltage; crossings between domains in different states need special cells:

Cell Where Job
isolation output of a domain that can power down clamp the floating output to a known 0/1
level shifter between domains at different voltages translate logic levels
retention register in a domain that powers down but keeps state tiny always-on shadow latch preserves the value
power switch in the domain’s supply the sleep transistor, under UPF control
always-on logic control, retention, isolation enables must stay powered to manage the rest

A power state table (PST) specifies allowed domain states. Verify isolation, voltage translation, retention, and power sequencing for each supported mode.

Energy vs. Power

Quantity Definition Bounds
power energy per time (W) thermal/cooling limit, supply current
energy power × time (J) battery life, cost per computation

Higher speed can reduce energy if finishing sooner permits sleep; lower voltage reduces switching energy but may extend runtime. Optimize joules per task under power and latency limits; energy-delay product (EDP) combines energy and delay.
